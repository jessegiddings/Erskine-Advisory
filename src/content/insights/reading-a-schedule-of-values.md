Somewhere in your project's first weeks, a spreadsheet arrives that will govern every payment you make for the next two years. It is called the schedule of values: the contract price broken into line items — excavation, foundations, framing, mechanical, finishes — each with a dollar figure. Every payment application afterwards claims a percentage complete against each line, and money moves accordingly.

Owners wave it through as paperwork. Contractors do not, because whoever shapes this document shapes the project's cash flow — and it is negotiated exactly once, at the moment the owner is least equipped to argue with it.

## Front-loading, the standard move

Front-loading assigns generous values to early line items — mobilization, excavation, foundations — and lean values to late ones. Each line is defensible in isolation; the pattern is the tell. The effect: the contractor is overpaid relative to work in place for most of the project, financing their operation on your money, and your remaining leverage shrinks with every draw.

The damage arrives at the worst moment. If the relationship fails at month eighteen, you discover you've paid for eighty percent of a house that is sixty percent built — and the twenty-point gap is your negotiating position, gone. Every stalled project we've been asked to look at has this arithmetic somewhere near the bottom of it.

What resists it is boring and effective: line items benchmarked against an independent cost opinion before signing, mobilization capped at a defensible figure, and vague catch-all lines ("general conditions" hiding a tenth of the contract) broken into parts that can be verified.

## A payment application is a claim

Once construction starts, the monthly payment application asserts percentages complete against each line. The person preparing it works for the party being paid. On most private projects, nobody independent ever checks the claim against the site before the money moves.

Verification is not exotic. Someone construction-literate and independent walks the site with the application in hand and asks, line by line: is the claimed work in place? Are the "materials stored" actually on site, properly stored, and yours if things go wrong? Do milestone claims match milestone definitions in the contract? Then a written recommendation — release, hold, or release-in-part — before the payment deadline.

## Why the discipline compounds

The first verified draw usually recovers little; five percent here, a storage question there. The value is behavioural. Applications prepared for someone who checks are different documents from applications prepared for someone who doesn't — more accurate, more conservative, better supported. After a few cycles the corrections mostly stop being needed, which is the point: the cheapest dispute is the one that's no longer worth submitting.

Holdback and retainage — the percentage withheld from each draw — deserve the same attention. They're jurisdiction-specific, they're your closeout leverage, and releasing them early because things are going well is among the most common and least reversible owner errors.

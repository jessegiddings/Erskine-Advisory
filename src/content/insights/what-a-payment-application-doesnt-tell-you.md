Once a month for the life of your project, a document arrives asking for money. It has a professional format — often an industry-standard form — with line items, percentages complete, and a certification block. It looks like an accounting statement. It is not one. A payment application is a *claim*, prepared by the party being paid, and everything about how it is built deserves the scrutiny you would give any invoice for several hundred thousand dollars written by its own beneficiary.

Here is what the form doesn't tell you.

## It doesn't tell you whether the schedule of values was honest

Every application claims percentages against the schedule of values — the contract price broken into line items, negotiated once, at the start, when most owners don't know to look. Front-loading is the standard move: generous values on early items (mobilization, excavation, foundations), lean values on late ones. Each line defensible in isolation; the pattern is the tell.

The effect is that the contractor runs overpaid relative to work in place for most of the project, and your leverage shrinks with every draw. If the relationship fails at month eighteen, you discover you've paid for eighty percent of a house that is sixty percent built — and the gap is your negotiating position, gone. Every stalled project we have been asked to examine has this arithmetic somewhere near the bottom of it. The fix is boring and effective: benchmark the line items against an independent cost opinion *before signing*, cap mobilization at a defensible figure, and break vague catch-all lines into parts that can be verified.

## It doesn't tell you whether the percentages are real

"Framing: 80% complete" is an assertion. The form provides no evidence, and the person who prepared it works for the party requesting the money. On most private projects, no one independent ever walks the site with the application in hand before payment. Verification is not exotic: line by line, is the claimed work in place? Do milestone claims match the milestone definitions in the contract? The questions are simple. What matters is that someone with no stake in the answer is asking them before the money moves, and issuing a written recommendation — release, hold, or release in part.

## It doesn't tell you what "materials stored" means

Applications routinely bill for materials purchased but not yet installed. Fair enough — until you ask: are the materials actually on site, or in a supplier's yard? Properly stored and insured? And if the contractor fails next month, are they *yours* — paid for, identified, and segregated — or an unsecured claim in someone's insolvency? Stored-materials lines deserve physical verification and paper: invoices, bills of sale, insurance confirmation. The application form asks for none of that.

## It doesn't tell you what happened to retainage

The percentage withheld from each draw — holdback in Canada, retainage in the US — is jurisdiction-specific, lien-connected, and your closeout leverage: it is what gets deficiencies finished when everyone is tired. Applications will, entirely legitimately, request its release as milestones pass. Releasing it early because things are going well is among the most common and least reversible owner errors. Know your jurisdiction's rules, and treat every release request as a decision, not a formality.

## Why checking changes behaviour

The first verified draw usually recovers little — a few points here, a storage question there. The value compounds: applications prepared for someone who checks are different documents from applications prepared for someone who doesn't. More accurate, more conservative, better supported. After a few cycles the corrections mostly stop being needed, which is the point. The cheapest dispute is the one no longer worth submitting.

Ask an owner mid-project how far over budget they are and you'll usually get a feeling, not a figure. Ask at the end and you get a figure that arrives as a shock — despite the owner having personally approved every dollar of it. That's the strange signature of change-order overrun: it happens *with* the owner's consent, one reasonable decision at a time.

## Why each change order is easy to approve

A change order arrives with a story, and the story is usually true. Ground conditions differed. The window supplier discontinued the line. You did ask for the better stone. Each item is modest against the contract price — what's $28,000 on a large project? — and each arrives with schedule pressure attached: *we can hold price and sequence if we know by Friday.*

So you approve it, as almost anyone would. The problem isn't the decision; it's that nobody is keeping the running total, converting each "modest" item into its share of a trend. Approved one at a time, changes are noise. Totalled monthly, they're a trajectory — and a trajectory can be steered.

## What change-order pricing lacks

Competition. The original bid was tensioned against other bidders; a change order is priced by a party that already has the job, under time pressure, for a customer who can't practically shop it. Nothing dishonest is required for those prices to drift upward — the structure does the work. This is also where scope quietly migrates: items that arguably belonged in the original contract price return as changes, and each one that passes unexamined teaches the estimator something about the next.

## The ledger

The countermeasure is almost embarrassingly simple. One document, current at all times:

- Every proposed, approved, and disputed change, with its cost and its schedule effect
- The running total against the contract price
- Contingency drawn versus contingency remaining — with the burn *rate*, not just the balance
- Which changes were owner-elected and which were project-driven — different categories, requiring different responses

Owner-elected changes are a budget conversation with yourself. Project-driven changes are a pattern to interrogate: clustered in one trade? Downstream of one design gap? Creeping back from the exclusions list? The ledger converts anecdotes into evidence while there's still time to act on it — and independent pricing review restores some of the tension competition used to provide. On the right terms (see [question four](/insights/questions-before-you-sign)), markup is fixed and cost is documented, which turns most pricing conversations into arithmetic.

## The quiet compounding

A change order's sticker price is rarely its whole price. Changes ripple — into adjacent trades, into schedule, into general conditions that bill by the month. A project that absorbs a hundred small changes hasn't absorbed a hundred independent costs; it has absorbed a hundred interacting ones. This is why the ledger tracks schedule effect alongside cost, and why "no schedule impact" on a change order form deserves the same scrutiny as the number.

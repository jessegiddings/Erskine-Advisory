This is written for the owner reading it late, after another update that didn't quite add up. Four months behind — or a budget that stopped meaning anything, or a site that has gone quiet on weekdays. First, the thing that is true and doesn't feel true tonight: the situation is almost certainly recoverable, and the next steps are more mechanical than they feel.

Second, the rule that governs everything else: **do not negotiate yet.** An owner who negotiates before establishing the facts negotiates from exhaustion, against a party with better information. Everything this week is about closing that information gap. Nothing this week is about confrontation.

## This week

**1. Stop the discretionary money.** Pay what is contractually due and verified — do not manufacture a breach by withholding properly earned payment — but pause optional approvals: pending change orders, allowance upgrades, early release of holdback. Every discretionary dollar released this week is leverage spent before you know your position.

**2. Assemble the file, quietly.** The contract and every amendment. Every signed change order, and the ones discussed but never signed. Every payment application and proof of payment. The original schedule and every revision you were shown. Key correspondence. You are not building a case; you are establishing what you actually agreed to, which is usually the first surprise.

**3. Write the timeline while you remember it.** Dates, promises, slippages, conversations. Memory degrades and disputes reward contemporaneous notes. An evening's work now is worth a great deal in six months, and it costs nothing if things resolve amicably — which they usually do.

**4. Establish the one number that matters: paid versus built.** What have you paid, and what is verifiably in place? Not the percentage in the last application — the percentage on the site. Most stalled projects have never honestly established this number, and every decision you will make — push, renegotiate, retender, exit — depends on it. This is where independent eyes earn their fee fastest, but even a rough honest cut beats the number in the paperwork.

**5. Ask the schedule question in writing.** Not "when will it be done" — you have heard that answer. Ask for a *recovery schedule*: the remaining scope, sequenced, with trade commitments behind the dates and the long-lead items called out. A contractor who can produce one has a plan and probably deserves the chance to run it. A contractor who responds with reassurance instead of a schedule has told you something too.

**6. Check the clocks.** Lien periods, notice provisions, holdback rules — jurisdiction-specific, deadline-driven, and indifferent to how busy you are. If a dispute is even possible, have construction counsel in the project's jurisdiction identify which clocks are running. This costs an hour of a lawyer's time and forecloses the worst outcomes.

## What not to do

Don't fire the builder this week — termination before the facts converts a slow project into an expensive dispute plus a slow project, and it is the one move that is very hard to reverse. Don't sign anything offered as a package deal that settles the past and the future in one signature. Don't vent in writing — every message you send this month may be read aloud later, so write like it. And don't take the site's word for the story, or your own anger's: take the file's.

We sell owner's representation, so discount everything below accordingly. But nobody in this category seems willing to write the other side of the argument, and it deserves writing — because representation is a real cost, some projects genuinely don't need it, and a firm that can't tell you when you don't need it shouldn't be trusted about when you do.

## You don't need one when the project is modest and you are close to it

A straightforward renovation. A builder you have direct, personal experience of — not references, experience. A site you can visit weekly without trying. Terms you actually understand. In that configuration, formal representation is overhead: the problems it exists to catch are ones you will catch yourself, at the kitchen table, before they compound. Spend a fraction of the fee on a construction lawyer's review of the contract before you sign; that alone puts you ahead of most owners.

## You don't need one if you truly have the expertise — and the hours

Some owners have run capital projects professionally. If you can read a schedule of values, price a change order, and challenge a delay claim, the only question left is time — and it is the question that catches people. Representation is not a skill so much as a standing discipline: hours per week, every week, for two or three years. If you have both the skill and the calendar, you are your own representative, and hiring one buys you convenience rather than protection. That can still be a rational purchase. It is not a necessary one.

## You don't need one when the existing oversight genuinely overlaps

Occasionally the structure already contains most of the checking: a rigorous lender's draw process, an architect with a real contract-administration scope and the appetite to enforce it, a fixed-price contract with almost no allowances on a simple site. Each of those protects someone else's interest first — the loan, the design — but on a simple project the overlap with your interest can be large enough that adding a representative buys little. The test is whether anyone independent is verifying work in place before payment. If someone competent truly is, the marginal value of a second verifier is small.

## When the answer flips

Be honest about the configuration, because it flips on specific facts rather than feelings:

- **The ratio that matters is complexity to attention, not budget to net worth.** A demanding career is precisely what makes self-representation fail quietly.
- **Distance.** If you cannot regularly stand on the site, every fact reaches you through a party with a position in it — [a different problem than most owners expect](/insights/building-from-a-distance).
- **Complexity.** Difficult sites, heavy engineering, factory-built scope, multiple prime contracts. Interfaces are where risk hides, and interfaces multiply.
- **A team that is new to you.** References are past tense. Verification is present tense.
- **Something already feels wrong.** Vague reporting, a quiet site, a budget nobody mentions. Trust the unease and get [an independent account of where things stand](/services/project-recovery) — now, not eventually.

## The one-question test

Would you commit the same capital to any other venture on the strength of reporting produced solely by the people receiving the money? If yes — genuinely, with a straight face — you don't need us. If no, the remaining question is scale: full representation, or a scoped [audit before you sign](/services/pre-construction-audit), which is where most of the value concentrates anyway.
